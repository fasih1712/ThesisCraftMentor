How to Choose a Dissertation Topic: 8 Practical Steps

By Thesis Craft Mentors · 26 September 2026 · 7 min read

Choosing a topic is the first big decision of your dissertation, and one of the most important. A good topic keeps you motivated for months, fits your deadline and gives you something genuinely new to say. A poor one can leave you stuck halfway through. Here is how to choose well.

What makes a good dissertation topic

Strong topics usually share four qualities:

  • Interesting to you. You will spend months on it. Curiosity is what gets you through the difficult weeks.
  • Relevant to your field. It connects to current debates, theories or problems in your discipline.
  • Focused. Narrow enough to answer properly within your word count and deadline.
  • Researchable. You can realistically get the data, sources or participants you need.

8 steps to choosing your topic

1. Check your requirements first

Read your module handbook before anything else. Note the word count, deadline, required methods, ethics approval rules and any topic restrictions. A brilliant idea that needs six months of fieldwork will not work for a twelve-week project.

2. Start from what you already enjoy

Look back at your lectures, essays and readings. Which topics did you enjoy? Which assignments got your best grades? Which questions did you keep thinking about after class? Your strongest dissertation ideas are often hiding in work you have already done.

3. Read recent research

Skim recent articles in the top journals of your field, especially the "limitations" and "future research" sections. Authors often tell you exactly what still needs to be studied. Review articles are also useful because they map an entire area in a single paper.

Tip: Keep a running list of every question that catches your attention while reading. After a week or two you will usually see a pattern in what interests you.

4. Narrow a broad area into a specific question

"Social media" is an area, not a topic. Narrow it step by step:

LevelExample
Broad areaSocial media
NarrowerSocial media and mental health
FocusedInstagram use and body image among young adults
Research questionHow does daily Instagram use relate to body-image satisfaction among university students aged 18 to 24 in the UK?

Each step adds a boundary: a platform, an outcome, a population, a place. By the end you have something you can actually investigate.

5. Check that sources and data exist

Before you commit, spend an hour searching Google Scholar and your library databases. If you find almost nothing, the topic may be too narrow or too new to support a literature review. If you find thousands of studies asking the same question, you will need a sharper angle. Also confirm you can access the data: surveys need willing participants, secondary data needs to be available, and interviews need people who will agree to talk.

6. Think about your method early

Your topic and your method go together. Ask yourself whether your question calls for numbers (surveys, experiments, secondary datasets), for depth (interviews, case studies, document analysis) or for both. Choose something you have the skills and time to carry out properly.

7. Make sure it is original enough

Originality at undergraduate or master's level does not mean a world-changing discovery. It can mean applying an existing theory to a new context, studying a different population, using a new dataset, or updating older research with recent evidence. That is usually enough.

8. Discuss it with your supervisor

Take two or three shortlisted ideas to your supervisor, each written as a one-line research question. They know what is feasible in your department and can point you towards key sources. Go in prepared and you will get far more useful feedback.

A quick test for your shortlist

For each idea, try to answer these questions in one sentence each:

  1. What exactly am I trying to find out?
  2. Why does it matter, and to whom?
  3. What data or sources will I use?
  4. Can I finish it before my deadline?

If you can answer all four clearly, you probably have a workable topic.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Choosing a topic that is too broad. "Climate change" cannot be covered in 10,000 words. One aspect, one place, one group can.
  • Following trends you do not care about. A fashionable topic is hard to sustain without real interest.
  • Ignoring practical limits. Access to data, ethics approval and time matter as much as the idea itself.
  • Waiting for the perfect idea. Topics sharpen as you read and write. Pick a solid one and refine it.
  • Changing topic too late. Small refinements are normal, but starting over halfway through is very costly.

Frequently asked questions

How early should I choose my dissertation topic?

As early as your programme allows. Many departments ask for a topic or proposal several months before submission. Starting early gives you time to read, refine your question and get ethics approval if needed.

Can I change my topic after I have started?

Usually yes, especially in the early stages, but speak to your supervisor first. Narrowing or reframing your question is common and often improves the dissertation.

Should my topic be related to my future career?

It can be a real advantage. A dissertation connected to the industry or field you want to enter gives you something concrete to discuss in job or PhD interviews.
