How to Write a Dissertation Introduction

By Thesis Craft Mentors · 2 November 2026 · 7 min read

The introduction is the first chapter your examiner reads, and it sets their expectations for everything that follows. A clear introduction tells them what you studied, why it matters and how you went about it, so they can read the rest of your dissertation with confidence.

In this guide

  2. What to include, step by step
  3. Length and when to write it
  4. A quick checklist before you submit

What the introduction needs to do

Your introduction is a map, not a summary of everything you found. It should answer four questions in a logical order:

  • What is this research about? The topic and the context it sits in.
  • Why does it matter? The problem, gap or puzzle that justifies the study.
  • What exactly will you do? Your aim, objectives and research questions.
  • How is the dissertation organised? A short guide to the chapters ahead.

If an examiner could read only your introduction and still explain what your project is trying to do and why, it has done its job.

What to include, step by step

Some departments prescribe a structure, so check your handbook first. If yours does not, the sequence below works for most subjects. Think of it as a funnel that moves from the broad context down to your specific study.

1. Background and context

Open with the wider setting of your topic, not with a dictionary definition or a sweeping statement such as "Since the dawn of time...". Start with something specific and relevant: a development in your field, a policy change, a trend or a debate. Two or three paragraphs are usually enough. Cite credible sources for any factual claim you make.

Example wording: "Remote working expanded rapidly in many UK organisations after 2020, and many have since adopted hybrid models permanently. Managers are now expected to lead teams they rarely see in person, yet there is limited research on how this affects the way new graduates build professional relationships."

Notice how the example moves from context to a specific group and then hints at a gap. That movement is what a good opening paragraph does.

2. The problem or gap

Explain what is missing, unresolved or contested. This is the heart of your rationale. Your gap might be a group that has been under-studied, a context that has not been explored, conflicting findings or a practical problem without a clear answer. You do not need to review the literature in depth here, because that is the job of your literature review chapter. Give just enough to show that the gap is real, and point forward to the later chapter.

3. Aim, objectives and research questions

State clearly what you set out to do. A common pattern is one overall aim, three or four objectives, and one to three research questions that match them. Keep the wording consistent with your proposal, unless your supervisor agreed changes.

ElementWhat it doesExample
AimThe overall purpose of the studyTo explore how hybrid working shapes the professional relationships of early-career employees
ObjectiveA specific step towards the aim, often starting with a verb such as "examine" or "evaluate"To examine how graduates describe their relationships with managers and peers
Research questionThe question the data will answerHow do early-career employees experience relationship-building in hybrid teams?

Make sure each objective can be traced to a question, and each question can realistically be answered with the methods you chose. If you are still refining yours, our guide to writing a research proposal covers how to shape them.

4. Significance and scope

Say who benefits from your findings: researchers, practitioners, policymakers or a particular profession. Be modest and realistic. "This study contributes a small-scale, exploratory view of..." is more credible than claiming to transform an entire field. Then set boundaries: the population, time period, location or theoretical lens you focus on, and what you deliberately leave out. Defining scope early protects you from criticism later, because examiners can see that omissions were a choice rather than an oversight.

5. A brief note on method

One short paragraph is enough: the broad approach (for example a qualitative study using semi-structured interviews), the type of participants or data, and a pointer to the methodology chapter. Do not repeat the full detail. If you need guidance on that chapter, see our guide to the dissertation methodology chapter.

6. Structure of the dissertation

Finish with a roadmap in a short paragraph or a few sentences, describing each chapter in one line. Use the future or present tense consistently, for example: "Chapter 2 reviews the literature on... Chapter 3 explains the methodology..." For a reminder of what each chapter should contain, see our chapter-by-chapter structure guide.

Length and when to write it

As a rough guide, the introduction is often around 8 to 12 per cent of the total word count. In a 10,000 word dissertation that is about 800 to 1,200 words. Your department may set its own expectation, so check the handbook and marking criteria.

Many students find it easiest to write a rough introduction early, to clarify their thinking, and then rewrite it once the findings and discussion are complete. By the end you know exactly what your dissertation argues, so the final introduction can promise precisely what the later chapters deliver. Whatever you do, read the introduction and conclusion side by side at the end. The aim and questions you set up at the start should be the same ones you answer at the finish.

A quick checklist before you submit

Read your finished introduction and tick off each question below. If you cannot answer one confidently, that is the paragraph to revise.

  1. Can I state the aim in one sentence? If it takes a paragraph, it is probably too vague.
  2. Is the gap clear within the first page or two? A reader should not have to hunt for the reason the study exists.
  3. Do my objectives and questions line up? Each one should connect to the others, and each should be answered in your discussion or conclusion.
  4. Have I defined key terms? Briefly explain any concept that could be read in more than one way, such as "engagement" or "sustainability", and say how you use it.
  5. Are my claims supported? Every statistic, trend or factual statement needs a credible, correctly referenced source. If you are unsure of your referencing style, our Harvard referencing guide is a good place to check the format.
  6. Does the roadmap match the final chapter order? Update it whenever you restructure.

It also helps to ask a friend outside your subject to read the first page. If they can explain your topic and why it matters, your writing is accessible. If they are confused, your examiner may be too, so simplify long sentences and cut unnecessary jargon.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Starting too broadly. Opening with global history or a dictionary definition wastes your most valuable words.
  • No clear gap. Describing a topic is not the same as justifying a study. Examiners want to know why this research is needed.
  • Mismatched aims and questions. Objectives that do not connect to the questions, or questions your methods cannot answer.
  • Overpromising. Claiming your small study will "prove" something or solve a major problem.
  • Giving away or repeating too much. Reporting detailed findings, or copying paragraphs from the literature review.
  • Forgetting the roadmap, or describing chapters that no longer match the final structure after you reorganised.
  • Leaving it unrevised. An introduction written in week one and never updated often describes a different dissertation from the one you submitted.

Frequently asked questions

Should the introduction include a literature review?

Not a full one. Use a few well-chosen sources to set the context and show the gap, then leave the detailed critical discussion for your literature review chapter. If you are working on that chapter, our guide to writing a literature review explains how to structure it.

Should I write the introduction first or last?

Both approaches are valid. A draft at the start helps you commit to an aim and a structure, while a final rewrite at the end ensures it matches what you actually found. Most students do both: a working draft early and a polished version last.

Can I use "I" in the introduction?

It depends on your discipline and department. Some fields welcome first-person phrases such as "I argue" or "I interviewed", especially in qualitative work, while others prefer impersonal wording such as "this study examines". Check your handbook or ask your supervisor, and stay consistent throughout.
