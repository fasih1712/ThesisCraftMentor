How to Write a Research Proposal That Gets Approved

By Thesis Craft Mentors · 29 September 2026 · 8 min read

A research proposal is your plan for the whole project. It shows your supervisor or admissions panel what you want to study, why it matters and how you will do it. A clear, well-structured proposal gets approved faster and makes the dissertation itself much easier to write.

  2. The standard structure, section by section
  3. Tips for a proposal that stands out

What a research proposal needs to do

Whether it is for an undergraduate dissertation, a master's thesis or a PhD application, every proposal must convince the reader of three things:

  • The question is worth asking. It addresses a real gap or problem.
  • You know the field. You understand the key research and where your study fits.
  • The plan is realistic. Your methods, timeline and resources can actually deliver an answer.

The standard structure, section by section

Always follow your department's template if there is one. If not, this structure works for most disciplines:

1. Title

Make it specific and descriptive. A reader should understand your topic, population and focus from the title alone. You can refine it later.

2. Introduction and background

Introduce the topic, give the essential context and explain why it matters now. End this section by stating the problem your research will address.

3. Research aims, objectives and questions

State one overall aim, then break it into two to four specific objectives or research questions. Each one should be clear, focused and answerable with your chosen method.

Example: Aim: to explore how small businesses in Lahore adopt digital payment systems. Question 1: What factors encourage adoption? Question 2: What barriers do owners report?

4. Literature review

This is a shorter version of a full literature review. Summarise the key theories and studies, show how they relate to each other and identify the gap your research will fill. Focus on the most relevant and recent work rather than trying to cover everything. Our literature review guide explains how to synthesise sources properly.

5. Methodology

Explain how you will answer your questions. Cover:

  • Research design: qualitative, quantitative or mixed methods, and why.
  • Data collection: surveys, interviews, experiments, secondary data or documents.
  • Sample: who or what you will study, how many and how you will select them.
  • Data analysis: for example statistical tests, thematic analysis or content analysis.
  • Ethics: consent, confidentiality, data storage and any approval you need.

6. Expected contribution

Briefly explain what your research will add: new evidence, a new context, practical recommendations or a contribution to theory. Be confident but realistic.

7. Timeline

Show your plan week by week or month by month. A simple table is enough:

StageTiming
Literature review and ethics approvalWeeks 1 to 4
Data collectionWeeks 5 to 9
Data analysisWeeks 10 to 12
Writing and first full draftWeeks 13 to 16
Revisions, proofreading and submissionWeeks 17 to 18

8. References

List every source you cited, in your required referencing style. A tidy, correctly formatted reference list signals care and attention to detail.

Tips for a proposal that stands out

  • Keep every section connected. Your gap, questions and methods should clearly lead into each other.
  • Justify your choices. Do not just say what you will do; explain why it is the right approach.
  • Be specific. "I will interview 12 small-business owners" is stronger than "I will interview some people".
  • Acknowledge limitations. Showing you understand the weaknesses of your design builds credibility.
  • Write clearly. Short sentences and plain language are easier for busy reviewers to follow.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Questions that are too broad to answer within the word count or time available.
  • A vague methodology with no detail on sample, data or analysis.
  • No clear gap, leaving the reader unsure why the study is needed.
  • An unrealistic timeline that ignores ethics approval or recruitment delays.
  • Ignoring the word limit. Going far over or under suggests poor planning.

Frequently asked questions

How long should a research proposal be?

It depends on the purpose. Undergraduate and master's proposals are often 1,000 to 3,000 words, while PhD proposals are commonly 2,000 to 5,000 words. Always check your university's guidance.

Can I change my research after the proposal is approved?

Yes, within reason. Research often evolves as you read and collect data. Minor changes are normal; larger ones should be discussed with your supervisor.

What tense should I use?

Use the future tense for what you plan to do ("this study will interview...") and the present or past tense when discussing existing literature.
