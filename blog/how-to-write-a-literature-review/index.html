How to Write a Literature Review for Your Thesis: A Step-by-Step Guide

By Thesis Craft Mentors · 23 September 2026 · 9 min read

The literature review is where many theses stall. You have read dozens of papers, your notes are everywhere, and turning all of it into a clear, argued chapter feels impossible. This guide breaks the job into steps you can actually follow.

In this guide
  1. What a literature review is (and isn't)
  2. 7 steps to writing your literature review
  3. How to structure the chapter
  4. Common mistakes to avoid
  5. Frequently asked questions

What a literature review is (and isn't)

A literature review is a critical, organised account of what is already known about your topic. Its job is to show your examiner three things:

  • You understand the key theories, debates and studies in your field.
  • You can judge the strengths and weaknesses of that research.
  • There is a clear gap that your own study sets out to fill.

It is not a list of summaries. "Smith (2019) found X. Jones (2021) found Y." is an annotated bibliography, not a review. A good review connects sources, compares them and builds an argument that leads straight into your research questions.

7 steps to writing your literature review

1. Pin down your scope

Start from your research question, not from the library. Write down in one or two sentences what your thesis investigates, then decide your boundaries: which time period, which regions or populations, which disciplines. A tight scope keeps you from drowning in marginally relevant papers.

2. Search systematically

Use your university's databases (Scopus, Web of Science, JSTOR, PubMed, ProQuest or the ones specific to your field) alongside Google Scholar. Build a list of keywords and synonyms and combine them with AND / OR. Keep a simple log of which searches you ran and when. If your supervisor asks how you found your sources, you will have an answer, and for systematic reviews it is essential.

Tip: Once you find a highly relevant paper, check its reference list (backward searching) and use "Cited by" in Google Scholar (forward searching). This often finds the best sources faster than keyword searches.

3. Read strategically and take structured notes

You cannot read everything in full. Read the abstract and conclusion first; only read the whole paper if it is clearly relevant. For each source, record the same fields every time, for example in a spreadsheet or a reference manager like Zotero or Mendeley:

FieldWhat to note
CitationFull reference in your required style
Aim / questionWhat the study set out to find
MethodDesign, sample, data, context
Key findingsThe two or three results that matter to you
LimitationsWeaknesses the authors admit, and ones you notice
ThemeWhich part of your review it belongs to

4. Group sources into themes

Look across your notes for patterns: recurring concepts, competing theories, methodological camps, or findings that agree and disagree. These clusters become the sections of your review. Most strong reviews are organised thematically rather than source by source or purely chronologically.

5. Synthesise, don't summarise

Synthesis means putting sources in conversation with each other. Compare this:

Summary: "Ahmed (2020) found remote work increased productivity. Lee (2022) found it decreased productivity."

Synthesis: "Evidence on remote work and productivity is mixed. Studies of knowledge workers report gains (Ahmed, 2020), while research on collaborative, team-based roles points to losses (Lee, 2022), suggesting that job type may moderate the effect."

The second version tells the reader what the research means together, which is what examiners reward.

6. Identify the gap

As you synthesise, note what is missing: a population nobody has studied, a method nobody has tried, contradictory findings nobody has explained, or evidence that is outdated. State the gap explicitly near the end of the chapter and link it directly to your research questions. This is the bridge into your methodology.

7. Write, then revise for argument

Write a rough draft section by section, then read it back asking one question: does every paragraph move my argument forward? Open each paragraph with a topic sentence that makes a claim, support it with sources, and close by linking to the next point. Finally, check that every in-text citation appears in your reference list and follows your required style (APA, Harvard, MLA, Chicago or other).

How to structure the chapter

  1. Introduction: the topic, why it matters, the scope of the review and how it is organised.
  2. Body: thematic sections, each synthesising the relevant research and ending with what it means for your study.
  3. Conclusion: the key takeaways, the gap in the literature, and how your research addresses it.

If your field uses a conceptual or theoretical framework, it usually appears either as its own section of the review or directly after it.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Listing instead of arguing. Each paragraph should make a point, not describe a paper.
  • Relying on old sources. Include foundational work, but make sure most of your sources are recent, typically from the last five to ten years depending on your field.
  • Over-quoting. Paraphrase and synthesise; save direct quotes for definitions or particularly powerful statements.
  • Ignoring conflicting evidence. Examiners notice when you only cite studies that agree with you.
  • Losing track of references. Use a reference manager from day one; fixing citations at the end takes days.
  • No clear gap. If the reader finishes the chapter unsure why your study is needed, the review hasn't done its job.

Frequently asked questions

How long should a thesis literature review be?

It varies by level and field, but a common guide is around 20 to 30 percent of the total word count. For a 15,000-word master's dissertation, that means roughly 3,000 to 4,500 words. Always check your department's handbook and ask your supervisor.

How many sources do I need?

There is no fixed number. Undergraduate dissertations often cite 20 to 40 sources, master's theses 40 to 80, and PhD theses considerably more. Quality and relevance matter more than the count.

Should I write the literature review first?

Usually you draft it early because it shapes your research questions and methods, but you should revisit and update it near the end so it reflects your final findings and any new publications.

What is the difference between a literature review and a systematic review?

A standard thesis literature review is a narrative, critical overview of relevant research. A systematic review follows a strict, pre-defined protocol for searching, screening and appraising studies (often following PRISMA guidelines) and is a research method in its own right.
