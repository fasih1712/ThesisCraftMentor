Dissertation Structure: What Goes in Each Chapter

By Thesis Craft Mentors · 8 October 2026 · 9 min read

Most dissertations follow the same basic shape, whatever the subject. Once you know what each chapter is for and roughly how long it should be, the project stops feeling like one enormous document and becomes a series of manageable pieces you can plan, draft and finish one at a time.

The standard dissertation structure at a glance

The structure below suits most empirical dissertations in the social sciences, business, education, health and similar fields. Always check your module handbook first: some departments ask for a combined "results and discussion" chapter, and humanities dissertations are often organised by theme instead.

The word counts are a typical split for a 12,000 word dissertation. Scale them up or down for your own limit.

SectionShare of wordsFor 12,000 words
Introductionabout 10%1,200
Literature reviewabout 25 to 30%3,000 to 3,600
Methodologyabout 15%1,800
Results or findingsabout 15 to 20%1,800 to 2,400
Discussionabout 20%2,400
Conclusionabout 5 to 10%600 to 1,200
Good to know: the abstract, reference list and appendices usually do not count towards the word limit, but rules vary. Check before you start cutting.

Front matter: title page, abstract and contents

Title page

Your title, name, student number, degree, department, supervisor and submission date. Many universities provide a template; use it exactly.

Declaration and acknowledgements

A signed statement that the work is your own, followed by an optional short thank-you to your supervisor, participants and anyone who helped. Keep acknowledgements to a paragraph or two.

Abstract

A summary of the whole dissertation in 200 to 300 words. In one paragraph, cover the problem, your aim, the method, the key findings and what they mean. Write it last, when you know exactly what you found.

Table of contents and lists

List every chapter and main heading with page numbers, followed by lists of tables and figures if you have them. Use your word processor's heading styles so the contents page updates itself.

The main chapters, one by one

Chapter 1: Introduction

The introduction tells the reader what the dissertation is about and why it matters. It should include:

  • Background and context: the wider topic and why it is important now.
  • The problem: what is unknown, unresolved or poorly understood.
  • Aim, objectives and research questions: stated clearly, ideally as a short numbered list.
  • Significance: who benefits from the answer, for example practitioners, policymakers or other researchers.
  • Structure of the dissertation: one or two sentences on what each following chapter does.

Chapter 2: Literature review

The literature review shows what is already known and where your study fits. Organise it by theme or debate rather than summarising one source after another, compare and evaluate studies, and finish by identifying the specific gap your research addresses. Our literature review guide walks through this step by step.

Chapter 3: Methodology

The methodology explains how you answered your research questions and, crucially, why you chose that approach. Cover your research design, sampling, data collection, analysis, ethics and limitations. We cover every part in detail in how to write a methodology chapter.

Chapter 4: Results or findings

Present what you found, without interpreting it yet. For quantitative work, this means descriptive statistics, test results, tables and charts. For qualitative work, it usually means themes supported by short quotations from your data. Organise the chapter around your research questions so the reader can see each one being answered.

Chapter 5: Discussion

This is where you explain what your findings mean. Compare them with the studies from your literature review: where do they agree, where do they differ, and why might that be? Discuss implications for theory and practice, and be honest about limitations. Strong discussion chapters are often what separates a good mark from a very good one.

Chapter 6: Conclusion

Answer each research question directly and briefly, summarise your contribution, and suggest recommendations and directions for future research. Do not introduce new data or new sources here.

Back matter: references and appendices

Reference list

Every source you cited, formatted consistently in your required style, whether that is Harvard, APA or another system. Small errors here are easy marks to lose, so leave time to check every entry.

Appendices

Supporting material that would interrupt the main text: survey questionnaires, interview guides, consent forms, ethics approval letters, extra tables or coding frameworks. Label them (Appendix A, Appendix B) and refer to each one in the main text.

How the chapters connect

Examiners look for a dissertation that reads as one argument, not six separate essays. A simple way to check this is to follow your research questions through the document:

  • The introduction states them.
  • The literature review shows why they need answering.
  • The methodology explains how you answered them.
  • The results present the answers.
  • The discussion explains what the answers mean.
  • The conclusion sums them up and looks ahead.

If a section does not serve at least one of your questions, it probably belongs in an appendix or nowhere at all.

Frequently asked questions

Which chapter should I write first?

Very few students write in order. A common approach is to draft the literature review and methodology first, then the results and discussion, and finally the introduction, conclusion and abstract, once you know what the dissertation actually argues.

Can I combine the results and discussion?

Yes, if your department allows it. Combined chapters are common in qualitative research, where interpreting each theme as you present it reads more naturally. Check your handbook or ask your supervisor.

Does a literature-based dissertation follow the same structure?

Not exactly. Desk-based or systematic review dissertations still need an introduction, methodology (explaining how you searched and selected sources) and conclusion, but the middle chapters are usually organised by theme rather than as results and discussion.
