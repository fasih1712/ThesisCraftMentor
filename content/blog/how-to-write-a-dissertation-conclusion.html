The conclusion is the last thing your examiner reads, so it shapes how they remember your work. A strong one is short, confident and clear: it answers your research questions, says what your study contributes and leaves the reader in no doubt about what you found. A weak one repeats the abstract, adds new material or fades out with an apology.

What a conclusion is for

Your conclusion closes the loop that your introduction opened. In the introduction you promised to investigate a problem; here you show what you found out and why it matters. A good conclusion does four things:

  • Summarises the main findings in a few clear sentences.
  • Answers each research question directly.
  • States the contribution your study makes to knowledge or practice.
  • Looks forward with recommendations and suggestions for further research.

Many examiners read the introduction and conclusion side by side to check that the project delivers what it set out to do. If the two do not match, that mismatch is noticed.

Conclusion or discussion?

Students often blur these two chapters. The discussion chapter is where you interpret results in depth and compare them with the literature. The conclusion is shorter and more decisive: it states what you can now say, not how you arrived there.

Discussion chapterConclusion chapter
Interprets findings in detailSummarises the key findings briefly
Compares results with the literatureDraws the overall message from that comparison
Explores explanations and alternativesGives clear answers to the research questions
Long and analyticalShort and focused

Some departments merge the two into a single "Discussion and conclusion" chapter, and some expect limitations in one place rather than the other. Check your handbook and marking criteria before you start.

A step-by-step structure

This sequence works for most dissertations. Adapt the order if your department provides a template.

1. Restate the aim and approach (two or three sentences)

Remind the reader what the study set out to do and how, without re-explaining the methodology. For example: "This dissertation set out to examine how first-year students use peer support when adjusting to university study. A qualitative design was used, drawing on semi-structured interviews."

2. Summarise the main findings

Pick the three or four most important findings and state them plainly. Do not list every result. Focus on the points that matter most for your research questions.

3. Answer the research questions

Take your questions one at a time and give a direct answer to each. This is the heart of the chapter, covered in the next section.

4. State the contribution

Say what your work adds, whether that is new evidence in a specific context, support for an existing model or a practical insight.

5. Acknowledge the limitations briefly

One short paragraph is enough. Name the main limits, such as sample size or a single setting, and say how they affect how far the findings can be generalised. You will usually have discussed these in more detail earlier.

6. Give recommendations and future research

Offer practical recommendations that follow from your evidence, then two or three specific suggestions for further study.

7. End with a closing statement

Finish with one or two sentences that state the overall message of your work. Avoid dramatic claims. A calm, confident ending is more convincing than a grand one.

Answering your research questions

The simplest way to stay on track is to quote or paraphrase each research question and answer it in a sentence or two. This makes it easy for the examiner to tick off that your project delivered.

Example: "The first research question asked how students describe their experience of peer support. The findings indicate that students valued informal, student-led support more than formal schemes, mainly because it felt less judgemental. The second question asked what barriers prevented students from using formal support. Participants most often mentioned limited awareness and timetable clashes."

This wording is illustrative only. Your answers must come from your own data and be consistent with your results and discussion chapters.

Use cautious, accurate language. Words such as indicate, suggest and are consistent with are usually safer than prove, unless your design genuinely supports a stronger claim. If a question could only be partly answered, say so honestly and explain why. That shows critical judgement.

Stating your contribution

Many students find this the hardest part, because they feel their project is too small to count as a contribution. At undergraduate and master's level, a contribution does not have to be groundbreaking. It can be:

  • Empirical: evidence from a particular group, place, sector or time period where little existed.
  • Theoretical: support for, or a challenge to, an existing framework.
  • Methodological: a useful way of measuring or exploring the topic.
  • Practical: findings that could inform how a service, organisation or policy works.

Look back at the gap you identified in your introduction and your literature review. The contribution is simply your statement of how far your study has filled that gap. Keep the claim proportionate: "adds evidence from a small regional sample" is honest and still valuable.

Length, tense and tone

  • Length: often around 5% to 10% of the total word count, so roughly 600 to 1,200 words in a 12,000 word dissertation. Word limits and expectations vary, so check your handbook.
  • Tense: past tense for what you did and found ("the study found"), present tense for what the findings mean now ("these results suggest").
  • Tone: formal, direct and confident, with measured claims.
  • Citations: keep them light. You may refer back to a key source, but the conclusion should mostly be your own voice.

If your departmental guidance says nothing, follow the pattern in your overall dissertation structure and ask your supervisor what they expect.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Introducing new material. No new data, sources or arguments belong in a conclusion. If it matters, it should have appeared earlier.
  • Repeating the abstract. A copy of the abstract is not a conclusion. Focus on answers, contribution and implications.
  • Overclaiming. Do not generalise beyond your sample or claim causation from correlation.
  • Apologising. Phrases such as "unfortunately this study was only a small project" weaken your work. State limitations factually and move on.
  • Vague recommendations. "More research is needed" says nothing. Name what kind of study would help and why.
  • Ignoring the research questions. If a question from the introduction is not answered, the examiner will notice.
  • Ending weakly. Do not trail off with a quotation or a general statement about the importance of the topic. End on your own finding.

A useful final check is to read your introduction and conclusion back to back. The aims, questions and promises in the first should be matched by clear answers in the second.

A quick checklist before you submit

Once your draft is complete, test it against these questions. If you can answer yes to each one, your conclusion is doing its job.

  • Does every research question have a clear, direct answer?
  • Do the answers match what your results and discussion chapters actually say?
  • Is the contribution stated honestly and in proportion to the size of the study?
  • Are the limitations acknowledged briefly, without apologising?
  • Are the recommendations specific and based on your evidence?
  • Could a reader who only read the introduction and conclusion understand what the project achieved?

Reading the chapter aloud is a surprisingly effective way to catch repetition, vague wording and sentences that promise more than the evidence supports. Ask a friend or your supervisor to read it too, and check whether they can summarise your main message in a single sentence. If they cannot, simplify.

Frequently asked questions

How long should a dissertation conclusion be?

It is usually short, often between 5% and 10% of the total word count. In a 12,000 word dissertation that means about 600 to 1,200 words. Your department's guidance takes priority over any general figure.

Can I include references in my conclusion?

Yes, but sparingly. You can mention a key source when you say how your findings relate to earlier work. The conclusion should mainly present your own answers and contribution, so avoid new sources that were not in the main body.

Should I write the conclusion last?

Most students draft it near the end, once the results and discussion are settled, then revise the introduction so the two match. A rough conclusion plan written earlier can still help you keep the project focused.
