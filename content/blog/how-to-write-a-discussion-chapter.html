The discussion chapter is where your dissertation stops reporting and starts thinking. Your results say what you found; the discussion explains what it means, why it matters and how it connects to what other researchers have already shown. It is also where many students lose marks, usually by repeating the results instead of interpreting them.

What the discussion chapter is for

Think of the discussion as your answer to the question every examiner is asking: "So what?" It should do four jobs:

  • Answer your research questions using the evidence from your results.
  • Interpret the findings, explaining what the patterns might mean and why they may have appeared.
  • Compare your findings with the literature you reviewed, showing where they agree, differ or add something new.
  • Evaluate the significance and the limits of your study honestly.

Some departments combine results and discussion in one chapter, particularly in qualitative work. Others keep them separate. Check your handbook, because the expected balance between description and interpretation changes depending on which you are writing.

A clear structure to follow

Your department may give a template. If not, the structure below works for most dissertations. The discussion is usually written in the past tense when referring to your own findings, and in the present tense for general claims about the literature.

1. A short opening paragraph

Remind the reader of your aim and research questions, and say how the chapter is organised. Keep it to a few sentences. Do not restate your whole methodology.

2. Discussion organised by research question or theme

This is the core of the chapter. Organise it around your research questions or your main themes, not around the order in which the data happened to appear. For each one:

  1. State the key finding in one or two sentences.
  2. Interpret it: what does it suggest, and what are the possible explanations?
  3. Compare it with previous research.
  4. Say what it adds or changes in our understanding.

3. Implications

Explain what your findings mean in practice and for theory. Practical implications might be for managers, teachers, clinicians or policymakers. Theoretical implications explain how your work supports, challenges or extends existing models. Keep your claims proportionate to your evidence.

4. Limitations

Acknowledge the main weaknesses of the study, such as a small or non-random sample, self-reported data, a single case or a short time frame. Then explain how these affect the conclusions you can draw. Some departments want this inside the discussion and others want a separate section or a place in the conclusion, so check which applies to you.

5. Recommendations for future research

Suggest two or three specific directions that follow from your findings and limitations. "More research is needed" is too vague; "a longitudinal study following the same graduates over three years would show whether this effect lasts" is useful.

How to interpret your findings

Interpretation is the skill that separates a strong discussion from a descriptive one. A helpful pattern is finding, meaning, evidence:

  • Finding: what the data showed.
  • Meaning: what you think it indicates and why.
  • Evidence: the literature or data that supports your explanation.
Example (descriptive, weaker): "Sixty per cent of respondents said they felt unsupported by their line manager."

Example (interpretive, stronger): "The majority of respondents reported feeling unsupported by their line manager. This suggests that the benefits of the flexible working policy may depend on managerial behaviour as much as on the policy itself, which is consistent with Smith's (2019) argument that implementation shapes outcomes."

The names and figures above are only an illustration of the wording. In your own chapter, cite sources you have actually read and numbers that come from your data.

Useful verbs for interpretation include suggests, indicates, is consistent with, may reflect and could be explained by. Avoid proves and shows conclusively unless your design genuinely allows it. Most studies, especially small ones, support cautious claims.

Linking back to the literature

Your literature review set up a conversation. The discussion is where you rejoin it. Return to the key studies and models you introduced and compare them with your results. Strong students do not just list agreement; they explain differences too.

If your finding...You can say...
Agrees with earlier researchIt supports and strengthens existing evidence, possibly in a new context, such as a different country or sector.
Partly agreesIt confirms one aspect but not another, and you can suggest what accounts for the difference.
Contradicts earlier researchIt challenges the established view, and you should consider differences in sample, method, time period or context.
Addresses a gap you identifiedIt offers new evidence where little existed, which is your contribution.

If you are unsure which sources to bring back, look at the gap you identified in your literature review and at the aims you set in your introduction. The discussion should close the loop on both.

Dealing with unexpected or weak results

Many students worry when results are not significant, contradict the hypothesis or are messy. This is normal, and a thoughtful discussion of unexpected results can earn as many marks as a neat confirmation. Examiners reward honest reasoning, not perfect outcomes.

  • Report them plainly. Do not hide or downplay results that did not go as planned.
  • Consider the explanations. Could the sample size, the measure used or the context have played a part? Is it possible the theory does not apply as expected?
  • Avoid overclaiming. A non-significant result does not prove there is no effect; it may mean your study could not detect one.
  • Turn it into a contribution. A surprising result often points to the most interesting future research.
Example wording: "Contrary to the hypothesis, there was no significant difference between the two groups. One possible explanation is the relatively small sample, which limited statistical power. Alternatively, the intervention may have been too brief to produce measurable change. Future studies with larger samples and a longer follow-up period would help to clarify this."

Building a strong discussion paragraph

If you are unsure how to begin, draft each paragraph with a simple sequence. Start with a topic sentence that states the point you are making. Present the relevant finding from your own data. Offer your interpretation and, where it helps, an alternative explanation. Then bring in one or two sources that agree or disagree, and finish with a sentence that says why this matters for your research question.

Read each paragraph back and ask: could someone who had not seen my results chapter still follow the argument? If the answer is no, add a brief reminder of the finding. If the paragraph is mostly a list of other people's studies, bring your own findings back to the centre. Your voice, backed by evidence, should lead the discussion, with the literature supporting it rather than replacing it.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Repeating the results. If a paragraph could sit unchanged in your findings chapter, it is not discussion yet. Interpret, compare and explain.
  • Introducing new results. New data or analysis belongs in the results, not here.
  • Forgetting the literature. A discussion with no references to earlier studies is incomplete.
  • Overclaiming. Generalising from a small sample or claiming causation from correlational data.
  • Ignoring the research questions. Readers should be able to see clearly where each question is answered.
  • Hiding limitations. Acknowledging weaknesses shows critical thinking. Pretending they do not exist does the opposite.
  • Drifting into the conclusion. Save the final summary and overall message for your conclusion chapter.

It also helps to check that your discussion matches your plan from the overall dissertation structure, and that the claims you make are supported by what you described in your methodology.

Frequently asked questions

How long should a discussion chapter be?

It is commonly one of the longer chapters, often around 15% to 20% of the total word count, so roughly 2,000 to 2,500 words in a 12,000 word dissertation. Word limits and weightings differ between departments, so check your handbook and marking criteria.

What is the difference between the discussion and the conclusion?

The discussion interprets your findings in detail, compares them with the literature and explores implications. The conclusion is shorter: it summarises the main answers to your research questions, states your contribution and ends with final recommendations. It should not introduce new arguments.

Can I combine the results and discussion chapters?

Sometimes, yes. Qualitative studies in particular often present findings and discussion together, theme by theme. Ask your supervisor or check your handbook before you decide, because some departments expect separate chapters.
