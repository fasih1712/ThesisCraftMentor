Harvard is the most widely used referencing style in UK universities. It has two parts that always work together: a short citation in your text, and a full entry in the reference list at the end. Get the pattern right once and you can reference almost any source in minutes.

Check your university's version first. "Harvard" is a family of styles, not one fixed standard. Punctuation, capitalisation and the use of "et al." vary between universities. The examples below follow the widely used Cite Them Right version. If your department has its own guide, follow it exactly.

How Harvard referencing works

Harvard is an author-date system. Whenever you use someone else's idea, data or words, you give the author's surname and the year of publication in brackets. The reader then finds the full details in your reference list, which is arranged alphabetically by surname.

You need to reference when you quote directly, paraphrase or summarise someone's ideas, or use their data, images or tables. You do not need to reference common knowledge, such as the fact that London is the capital of the UK.

In-text citations

Basic format

Put the surname and year in brackets, usually at the end of the sentence before the full stop:

Thematic analysis is one of the most widely used methods in qualitative research (Braun and Clarke, 2006).

If the author's name is part of your sentence, put only the year in brackets:

Braun and Clarke (2006) describe six phases of thematic analysis.

Direct quotations

When you quote word for word, use quotation marks and add the page number:

Thematic analysis is "a method for identifying, analysing and reporting patterns (themes) within data" (Braun and Clarke, 2006, p. 79).

Use direct quotes sparingly. Paraphrasing in your own words, with a citation, shows better understanding and usually earns more credit.

Several sources at once

Separate them with semicolons, usually in date order: (Bryman, 2016; Saunders, Lewis and Thornhill, 2019).

Two works by the same author in the same year

Add a lower-case letter after the year, both in the text and in the reference list: (Smith, 2023a) and (Smith, 2023b).

Secondary referencing

If you read about a study in another source but did not read the original, cite both and list only the source you actually read: (Maslow, 1943, cited in Robbins and Judge, 2019). Try to find and read the original wherever possible.

The reference list

  • Put it at the end of your work under the heading Reference list or References.
  • Arrange entries alphabetically by the author's surname, or by organisation name.
  • Include every source you cited in the text, and nothing you did not cite. A list of background reading is a bibliography, which some departments ask for separately.
  • Use the same format for every entry of the same type. Consistency is what markers check most.

Examples for common source types

Book

Format: Surname, Initial(s). (Year) Title. Edition (if not the first). Place of publication: Publisher.

Saunders, M., Lewis, P. and Thornhill, A. (2019) Research methods for business students. 8th edn. Harlow: Pearson.
Creswell, J.W. and Creswell, J.D. (2018) Research design: qualitative, quantitative, and mixed methods approaches. 5th edn. Thousand Oaks, CA: SAGE.

Chapter in an edited book

Format: Chapter author surname, Initial(s). (Year) 'Chapter title', in Editor initial(s). Surname (ed.) Book title. Place of publication: Publisher, pp. first page–last page.

Journal article

Format: Surname, Initial(s). (Year) 'Article title', Journal Title, volume(issue), pp. page range. Available at: DOI or URL.

Braun, V. and Clarke, V. (2006) 'Using thematic analysis in psychology', Qualitative Research in Psychology, 3(2), pp. 77–101. Available at: https://doi.org/10.1191/1478088706qp063oa.

Include the DOI whenever the article has one. It is a permanent link, so it is preferred over a database URL.

Web page

Format: Author or organisation (Year) Title of page. Available at: URL (Accessed: day month year).

If no individual author is named, use the organisation that published the page. If there is no date, write "(no date)". Always include the date you accessed it, because web pages change.

Report

Format: Organisation or author (Year) Title of report. Place of publication: Publisher. Available at: URL (Accessed: day month year).

Lecture slides or notes

Format: Lecturer surname, Initial(s). (Year) 'Title of lecture' [Lecture], Module code: Module title. Institution. Date.

Many lecturers prefer you to cite the published sources behind their slides instead, so check before relying on lecture material.

AI tools such as ChatGPT

Universities treat AI tools very differently: some ban them for assessed work, some allow limited use with a declaration, and many have their own citation format. Check your university's current policy before using or citing any AI-generated content.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Citations with no matching reference, or references never cited in the text.
  • Inconsistent formatting, such as switching between "and" and "&", or italicising some titles but not others.
  • Missing page numbers on direct quotations.
  • Listing first names instead of initials in the reference list.
  • Copying citations from Google Scholar without checking them: auto-generated references often contain errors.
  • Leaving references to the end. Record full details as you read and you will save hours before submission.

Frequently asked questions

Should I use a reference manager?

Yes, especially for a dissertation. Free tools such as Zotero, or Mendeley and EndNote if your university provides them, store your sources and format citations automatically. Always check the output against your university's guide, as small errors are common.

Do references count towards my word limit?

The reference list usually does not, but in-text citations usually do. Check your module handbook, because rules differ between departments.

What is the difference between Harvard and APA?

Both are author-date styles, so they look similar in the text. The main differences are small: APA uses "&" between author names in brackets and a different order and punctuation in the reference list. Use whichever style your department requires, and never mix the two.
