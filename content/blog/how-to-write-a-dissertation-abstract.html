The abstract is the shortest part of your dissertation and often the hardest to write. It is usually the first thing an examiner reads and the only part many people will ever see, so it needs to say what you did, how you did it, what you found and why it matters, all in a few hundred words.

What an abstract is (and is not)

An abstract is a self-contained summary of your whole dissertation. Someone should be able to read it on its own and understand your question, your approach, your main results and your conclusion. It is not an introduction, a teaser or a list of topics. It does not promise findings; it reports them.

Because it summarises the finished work, write it last. You cannot summarise conclusions you have not yet reached, and your argument often shifts slightly between the first draft and the final chapter. An abstract written in week three rarely matches the dissertation you submit.

Length varies, so check your handbook. Many universities ask for around 150 to 300 words for undergraduate and taught master's dissertations, while some doctoral programmes allow more. Follow your department's limit exactly. Some also require the abstract on a separate page, keywords underneath, or a version in a second language, which is common at German universities.

The five elements of a strong abstract

Almost every good abstract answers the same five questions, in roughly this order.

ElementThe question it answersTypical share
Background and problemWhat is the context, and why does the study matter?1 to 2 sentences
Aim or research questionWhat did you set out to find out?1 sentence
MethodHow did you do it, and with whom or what data?2 to 3 sentences
FindingsWhat did you actually discover?2 to 4 sentences
Conclusion and implicationsSo what? What does it mean, and for whom?1 to 2 sentences

The findings are the part students most often skip. Writing "the results are discussed" tells the reader nothing. State the actual outcome, even if it is modest or mixed.

How to write it, step by step

1. Finish the dissertation first

Complete your discussion and conclusion before you start. Then reread your aim, research questions and conclusion chapter, because the abstract should mirror them.

2. Pull out one sentence from each chapter

Make a rough list: one sentence from your introduction (the problem and aim), one from the methodology, one or two from the findings and one from the conclusion. If you are unsure which sentences carry the weight, look at the first and last paragraphs of each chapter. Our guide to dissertation structure explains what each chapter should contribute, which makes this extraction easier.

3. Draft without worrying about length

Join the sentences into a paragraph and smooth the transitions. It will probably be too long. That is normal, and it is easier to cut than to expand.

4. Cut ruthlessly

Remove anything an examiner will not miss:

  • Background that does not directly motivate the question.
  • Detail about the literature review, which rarely belongs in an abstract.
  • Citations and references, unless your department asks for them.
  • Abbreviations and jargon that are not defined, tables, figures and quotations.
  • Phrases such as "this dissertation aims to" repeated several times.

5. Check it against the dissertation

Every claim in the abstract must appear in the main text. Do not add a result, a number or a conclusion that is not in your findings and discussion. If your abstract says you interviewed 12 participants, the methodology must say the same.

6. Add keywords if required

Many departments ask for three to six keywords. Choose terms a researcher would type when searching for your topic, such as the main concept, the method and the setting, rather than very general words.

A worked example

Here is a short informative abstract for an imaginary qualitative study. It is an illustration of structure and tone, not a real study.

Example abstract (about 170 words): Hybrid working has become common in UK workplaces, but little is known about how it affects the way new graduates build professional relationships. This study explores how early-career employees experience relationship-building in hybrid teams. A qualitative design was used, with semi-structured interviews with ten graduates employed in the first two years of their careers. Interviews were analysed using reflexive thematic analysis. Three themes were developed: the loss of informal contact, the growing importance of deliberate communication, and uneven access to senior colleagues. Participants who described explicit onboarding routines reported stronger relationships than those who relied on chance encounters in the office. The findings suggest that organisations cannot assume relationships will form informally and should structure opportunities for contact. Because the sample was small and drawn from one sector, the findings are exploratory and cannot be generalised, but they point to areas for larger studies.

Notice what it does well. Each sentence has a job, the method is specific enough to judge, the findings are stated rather than hinted at, and the limitation is acknowledged in one clause without overshadowing the contribution. If your analysis uses thematic analysis, you can find the right wording for that in your methodology chapter; our guide to the methodology chapter shows how to describe it.

Descriptive and informative abstracts

There are two broad types. A descriptive abstract says what topics the work covers without reporting results (for example, "This dissertation examines..."). An informative abstract summarises the problem, method, findings and conclusions, which is what the example above does. Most dissertations and theses expect the informative type. If your handbook is silent, choose informative, because it is more useful to readers and gives your examiner more confidence in your work.

Subject conventions differ too. Scientific and social science abstracts often follow a structured pattern with the headings background, methods, results and conclusions, while humanities abstracts are usually a single flowing paragraph focused on the argument. Look at abstracts from past dissertations in your department, or journal articles in your field, to see which style is normal.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Writing it too early. The abstract then promises an argument the dissertation no longer makes.
  • Describing instead of reporting. "Findings are presented and discussed" is a description; "participants who received structured onboarding reported stronger relationships" is a finding.
  • Copying sentences from the introduction. The abstract should be written for a reader who has not read the dissertation, so reuse of long passages usually reads badly and is often flagged by similarity software.
  • Too much background. Half the abstract spent on context leaves no room for results.
  • New information. Adding results or claims that are not in the main text.
  • Overclaiming. Words such as "proves" or "demonstrates conclusively" are rarely justified by a student project. Prefer "suggests", "indicates" or "provides evidence that".
  • Ignoring the word limit. Going over can cost marks, and a tight abstract shows control of your material.
  • Unexplained abbreviations and citations. The abstract must make sense on its own.

A final check before you submit

Read the abstract aloud once, then ask yourself four things. Could a stranger tell what you studied? Is the method clear enough to judge? Does it state at least one real finding? Does every number and claim match the main text? Finally, read it next to your conclusion chapter. If the two tell different stories, fix whichever is wrong before you submit. It also helps to leave it for a day and reread it with fresh eyes, because small errors in a short text are easy to miss.

Frequently asked questions

Should I write the abstract in the past or present tense?

Conventions vary, but a common approach is the past tense for what you did and found ("interviews were conducted", "three themes were developed") and the present tense for conclusions and implications ("the findings suggest"). Whichever you choose, be consistent and follow your department's guidance.

What is the difference between an abstract and an executive summary?

An abstract is a short, single-block summary used mainly in academic work. An executive summary is longer, often a page or two, and is aimed at decision-makers in business or professional reports, so it focuses on recommendations. Some master's courses require one, some the other, and some both. Check which your handbook asks for.

Can I include references in the abstract?

Usually not. Abstracts typically contain no citations, tables or figures. The exception is when your study directly evaluates or builds on one specific work, in which case a brief mention may be acceptable. If you do cite anything, make sure the format follows your required style, for example our Harvard referencing guide, and confirm the rule with your supervisor.
